The electric field of point charges

Three ideas, each with something to try. This is the whole of the reading for this lesson.

Idea 1

Fields add

When several charges are present, each one produces its own field exactly as if the others were not there. The net field at any point is the vector sum of the individual fields:

E→net=E→1+E→2+E→3+⋯

So to find the field at a point: work out the field of each charge there, direction and size, then add them as vectors.

Idea 2

The electric dipole

Two equal and opposite charges, +q and −q, a small distance s apart, form an electric dipole. Dipoles are everywhere: a water molecule is one, because its electrons sit slightly closer to the oxygen atom than to the hydrogen atoms.

A dipole is described by its dipole moment p→: a vector of magnitude p=qs that points from the negative charge to the positive charge.

Far from the dipole, at a distance r much larger than s, the field is

E→axis ≈14πε02p→r3 on the line through the two charges E→plane ≈−14πε0p→r3 in the plane that bisects the dipole

Two things to notice. At the same distance, the field on the axis is twice as strong as the field in the bisecting plane, and the two point in opposite directions. And both fall off as 1/r3, faster than the 1/r2 of a single point charge, because from far away the two opposite charges nearly cancel.

Idea 3

Electric field lines

Drawing an arrow at every point soon gets crowded. Field lines are a second way to picture the same field. They follow four rules:

  1. A field line is a continuous curve. At every point on it, the electric field vector is tangent to the line.
  2. Where the lines are closer together, the field is stronger. Where they are farther apart, it is weaker.
  3. Field lines start on positive charges and end on negative charges.
  4. Field lines never cross.

With both on, check rule 1: every arrow lies along the line that passes through it.

A prediction to bring to class

A platypus hunts underwater with its eyes, ears and nostrils shut. It finds shrimp by sensing, with its bill, the weak electric field that a shrimp's muscles create in the water around it.

What do the electric field lines around the shrimp look like? Draw them. Draw each line in the direction you think the field points; an arrowhead is added where you lift your finger.
